Explain it like I'm five

Think of a tall glass of iced lemon tea. A perfume is a bit like that glass, because you notice different things at different times. Perfumers call these layers notes.

First you smell the citrus, like a squeeze of lemon in the tea. It is bright and sharp. In Imagination that is citron, bergamot and orange. This part comes first, and it goes away first.

Next you smell the spice, like a little ginger or cinnamon in the cup. In Imagination the middle is ginger, cinnamon and neroli.

Last is the tea itself. It is dark and calm, and it comes last. In Imagination that is black tea, guaiac wood and incense.

There is one more helper. It is called ambroxan. Think of it as glue for smell. It is known for clinging to skin and fabric. That is the whole idea: a fresh glass of iced tea, with an ingredient that is meant to help the smell hold on.

What is actually in it

On paper, Imagination is an eau de parfum from France by perfumer Jacques Cavallier Belletrud, launched in 2021. These are the notes, from the Base Note scent catalogue:

  • Top (first minutes): citron, Calabrian bergamot, Sicilian orange.
  • Heart (next): Tunisian neroli, Nigerian ginger, Ceylon cinnamon.
  • Base (what stays): Chinese black tea, ambroxan, guaiac wood, olibanum (a type of incense).

Ambroxan. Per Wikipedia's entry on it, ambroxan smells woody, musky and slightly salty, a bit like ambergris. Ambergris is a waxy material that comes from sperm whales' digestive systems. Wikipedia's ambergris page says perfume makers prize it as a fixative, an ingredient that helps a scent last longer, and that makers moved to a synthetic version. The ambroxan page adds that it is very tenacious, meaning it holds on, and that it clings to skin and fabric. It was historically made from sclareol, a compound found in clary sage oil. The brand's own launch copy, as carried by The Stylemate (article dated 2 June 2021), describes the ingredient as a molecule found naturally in ambergris and praises it in very grand terms. That is marketing wording, not a test.

CO2 extraction. The same Stylemate page says the black tea, and the Nigerian ginger, are extracted with carbon dioxide. That is a way to pull smells out of a plant. Per Wikipedia's page on supercritical fluid extraction, carbon dioxide is the most common solvent for this, and spices and herbs are among the things it is used on. I am not claiming this makes the scent better: the source is the brand's copy, not independent testing.

Why people keep ordering it

Here is the evidence, from three places. Each outside page below was read on 2026-11-15.

Base Note's own orders. Louis Vuitton Imagination is the scent we have sold the most of. It sits at number 1 on the bestseller list in our Scent Catalog, which covers orders from March to November 2026, and we have sold more than 50 vials. That is one small shop's data, so read it as a signal and not a market ranking.

One other retailer's orders. Scento, a decant retailer, says its own Fragrance Index (orders, October 2025 to September 2026) ranks Imagination number 1 in Europe, and first in Germany, Italy, France and Greece (its own promotional page). Treat it as a company's own figure. It is not independently checked. Still, it is a second shop reporting a similar ranking.

What reviewers say.

  • A reviewer at ScentAdvice calls it "one of the most popular fresh niche fragrances" and an "easy reach" type of scent. The reviewer also says it has "an iced tea quality" and compares the opening to a few drops of citrus in ice-cold water. That is one reviewer's opinion, but it matches the lemon tea picture above.
  • The independent critic at Persolaise (29 August 2021) describes the base as ambery musks with a lot of ambroxan and a sea-spray tang. The reviewer finds the tea note serene, and adds that a heavy dose of ambroxan can look lazy, but is justified when it works within the whole scent.

Put together, the likely reasons are simple: it opens bright and it is easy to like. That is my reading of the evidence, not a measured fact.

The honest downsides

  • Generic, for the price. The ScentAdvice reviewer still thinks it is on the generic side, especially at a Louis Vuitton price, and calls it hard to recommend given its price and common scent.
  • Not a bold signature. Scento's review page says Imagination is built around transparency and is a weaker choice for anyone who wants a bold signature scent.
  • Familiar. Persolaise says the opening recalls a 1995 Cavallier scent, Bulgari Pour Homme, though not as a clone of it, and would have given this one a name that nods to a remake of an older idea.
  • Polarizing, maybe. Vivir, an article with only a site byline and no sources cited, says the heavy use of ambroxan may split opinion among traditionalists. I label it low confidence.
  • How long it lasts is unsettled. Scento's review page gives roughly 7 to 9 hours, depending on skin and weather. TheScentBase shows around 4 to 6 hours in its rating section, without saying how many ratings sit behind that figure, while the single review on the same page says about eight hours. In our Imagination versus Aventus comparison, the range across sources runs from about 4 to 5 hours up to 12 or more. Skin, number of sprays and nose fatigue all change the answer. I am not giving you a figure, because none of these sources settles it.

Bottle or vial

The bottle. About $320 to $360 for 100 ml, depending on the source, as read in October 2026 (details in the comparison post above). Those may not be today's prices. Glass Magazine (10 June 2021) notes that the bottle can be refilled at Louis Vuitton boutiques with perfume fountains.

The vial. A 5 ml vial of Imagination is $20 one-time at Base Note, and it holds about 60 sprays. We decant by hand, to order, from the original bottle, so it is the genuine fragrance.

The math.

  • Bottle: $320 divided by 100 ml is $3.20 per ml. At $360 it is $3.60 per ml.
  • Vial: $20 divided by 5 ml is $4.00 per ml. Spread over about 60 sprays, that is about 33 cents a spray.
  • Three vials at the cart price of $56: $56 divided by 15 ml is about $3.73 per ml.

So the vial costs more per ml than the bottle. What you are buying is a low-risk trial. A $20 test is cheaper than finding out a $320 to $360 bottle was not for you, but it is not a bargain on volume. If you wear it daily and already know you love it, the bottle is the cheaper route per ml.

Frequently asked questions

Does "viral" mean it has millions of views?

No. I found no source that states a view count, so I am not claiming one. Here it means popularity by orders: Base Note's own, and Scento's reported European data.

What does Imagination smell like?

On paper, bright citrus (citron, bergamot, orange) and ginger, then black tea, guaiac wood and incense over ambroxan. One reviewer compares the opening to citrus in ice-cold water.

How long does it last?

The sources disagree, from about 4 to 5 hours up to 12 or more. Test it on your own skin, with the same number of sprays each time, and ask someone near you.

Is a vial cheaper than the bottle?

Per ml, no: $4.00 for the $20 vial against about $3.20 to $3.60 for the bottle at the October 2026 prices. The vial is cheaper as a first step, because it costs far less up front.
