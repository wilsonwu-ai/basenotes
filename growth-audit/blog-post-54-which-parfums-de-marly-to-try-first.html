1. Parfums de Marly Layton: for people who like apple with lavender

Smells like: Apple, bergamot and cardamom on top; lavender, violet and geranium in the heart; patchouli, vanilla, guaiacwood and praline in the base.

Bergamot is a citrus fruit, the bergamot orange, whose extracts are used in perfumes. Wikipedia notes that oil of guaiac, an ingredient for soaps and perfumes, is produced from the wood of the Bulnesia sarmientoi tree. Praline is a confection of nuts, usually almonds, pecans and hazelnuts, with sugar. The house writes of "the freshness of apple" and of patchouli's "earthy depth". Source: Parfums de Marly.

Wear it for: A long Sunday lunch with friends.

How it differs from the other four: No other list here has apple, and none carries violet either; Pegasus lists "Violet Leaves", a different note name from Layton's "Violet". Cardamom sits in Layton's top notes, where Althair places it in the heart. In the base, patchouli shares space with vanilla and praline. None of the other four lists that combination: Haltane has patchouli and praline but no vanilla, and Althair has vanilla and praline but no patchouli.

2. Parfums de Marly Althair: for people who like cinnamon and orange blossom over vanilla

Smells like: Bergamot, mandarin and elemi on top; orange blossom, cinnamon and cardamom in the heart; bourbon vanilla, guaiacwood and praline in the base.

Elemi is the resin harvested from Canarium luzonicum, a tree native to the Philippines. Bourbon vanilla is a common name for Vanilla planifolia, taken from Île Bourbon, the former name of Réunion. On its own site the house spells the name Althaïr and closes its description with "Elegant, generous and charismatic." Source: Parfums de Marly.

Wear it for: The afternoon you finally spend in that bookshop café.

How it differs from the other four: Mandarin, elemi and cinnamon show up nowhere else on this page, and Althair alone names its vanilla bourbon vanilla. Orange blossom connects it to Pegasus. Its base of vanilla, guaiacwood and praline repeats three of Layton's four base notes and leaves out only patchouli.

3. Parfums de Marly Haltane: for people who like praline with leather

Smells like: Bergamot and pink peppercorn on top; saffron, praline, lavender and clary sage in the heart; oud, patchouli, vetiver and leather in the base.

Saffron is a spice made from the crocus flower, and Wikipedia describes its fragrance as hay-like. Clary sage is a herb in the Salvia genus whose distilled oil is used widely in perfumes. Oud is another name for agarwood, a resinous wood used in incense and perfume. Vetiver is a perennial bunchgrass that has been used to produce perfumes. The house begins its description with "the aromatic freshness of bergamot and lavender" and names a "gourmand praline heart"; a gourmand perfume is one built mainly on synthetic edible notes such as honey, chocolate, vanilla or candy. Source: Parfums de Marly.

Wear it for: Dinner at the wine bar you have been meaning to try.

How it differs from the other four: None of the other four lists pink peppercorn, saffron, clary sage, oud or leather, and Haltane alone carries four heart notes. Its list has no vanilla. Praline sits in the heart, while Layton and Althair keep praline in the base. Besides bergamot, vetiver is the note it shares with Perseus, and praline, lavender and patchouli are the notes it shares with Layton.

4. Parfums de Marly Perseus: for people who like grapefruit and vetiver

Smells like: Bergamot and grapefruit on top; geranium and blackcurrant bud in the heart; vetiver, amberwood and grey amber in the base.

Blackcurrant bud refers to the unopened buds of the blackcurrant, which are distilled for an essential oil used in perfumery. Amberwood and grey amber are note names the house prints without describing what they are. Its own summary says Perseus "combines bright grapefruit, fresh geranium, and refined vetiver". Source: Parfums de Marly.

Wear it for: When the plan is a morning walk to the farmers market.

How it differs from the other four: At seven notes, this is the shortest list of the five. Grapefruit, blackcurrant bud, amberwood and grey amber appear nowhere else here, and the list has no vanilla, praline or lavender. Geranium is the link to Layton; vetiver is the link to Haltane.

5. Parfums de Marly Pegasus: for people who like almond and anise

Smells like: Anise, bergamot and almond on top; cypress, violet leaves and orange blossom in the heart; ambrox, vanilla and sandalwood in the base.

Anise is a herb, and Wikipedia notes the word covers both the plant and its licorice-like flavour. Ambrox is another note name printed without a description. Parfums de Marly writes that Pegasus "opens with radiant bergamot" and brings in "the smoothness of almond". Source: Parfums de Marly.

Wear it for: Gallery openings.

How it differs from the other four: No other fragrance here lists anise, almond, cypress, violet leaves, ambrox or sandalwood, and Pegasus skips both praline and lavender. Orange blossom connects it to Althair. Vanilla connects it to both Layton and Althair.

How do three of these pairs differ on their notes?

Three pairings are below, compared only by what the official note lists say.

Layton vs Pegasus

Only two notes appear on both lists, bergamot and vanilla, and the rest of each pyramid goes its own way. Layton sets apple and cardamom beside the bergamot on top, holds lavender, violet and geranium in the heart, and surrounds its vanilla with patchouli, guaiacwood and praline. Pegasus puts anise and almond in the top, cypress, violet leaves and orange blossom in the heart, and ambrox and sandalwood next to its vanilla. Violet and violet leaves look close on paper, yet the two official pages print them under different names, and the Pegasus page lists no lavender at all.

Pick by this: if apple and lavender appeal more than almond and anise, start with Layton; if the reverse is true, start with Pegasus.

Althair vs Layton

No other pair among the five shares as much. Both lists carry bergamot, cardamom, vanilla, guaiacwood and praline, though Althair calls its vanilla bourbon vanilla. One shared note changes places: cardamom is a top note for Layton and a heart note for Althair. Layton alone brings apple, lavender, violet, geranium and patchouli. Althair alone brings mandarin, elemi, orange blossom and cinnamon.

Pick by this: if cinnamon and orange blossom appeal more than apple and lavender, start with Althair; otherwise, start with Layton.

Haltane vs Althair

This pair shares only bergamot and praline, and even the praline sits in different places: the heart for Haltane, the base for Althair. Haltane adds pink peppercorn, saffron, lavender, clary sage, oud, patchouli, vetiver and leather, and its list has no vanilla. Althair adds mandarin, elemi, orange blossom, cinnamon, cardamom, bourbon vanilla and guaiacwood. Their bases have nothing in common. Haltane's is oud, patchouli, vetiver and leather; Althair's is bourbon vanilla, guaiacwood and praline.

Pick by this: if saffron and leather appeal more than cinnamon and orange blossom, start with Haltane; if not, Althair is the one to try.

Where does the Parfums de Marly name come from?

The name points to a building. Wikipedia says the brand's name is inspired by the Château de Marly, a residence of King Louis XV. The same entry describes Parfums de Marly as a French niche perfume house, established in 2009 by Julien Sprecher and headquartered in Paris, and puts its range at over 30 fragrances.

Wikipedia also places the house in its century. It says the 18th century was considered the golden age of perfumery, that Louis XV was famed for leading the "perfumed court", and that the king was known for his love of racehorses.

Horses carry through to the emblem. On its story page, Parfums de Marly calls its modern blazons a tribute to the horses of Marly, which it describes as "Masterpieces commissioned in 1743 by Louis XV from the sculptor Guillaume Coustou to adorn the Château entrance."

The founder's intent is quoted on that page too. "I founded the Maison to express my own vision of modern Haute Parfumerie that disrupts the codes by taking inspiration freely from the spirit of 18th-century France," Sprecher says. Each of the five product pages follows its ingredient list with the same line: "All our perfumes are made in France."

How can you try these five side by side?

Compare two at a time, and choose pairs that share notes, because a shared note gives you something fixed to compare the differences against. Layton with Althair shares five listed notes, Layton with Haltane shares four, and Pegasus with Althair shares three. Put one fragrance on each wrist and write down which went where before you forget.

Smell both wrists soon after you put them on, and again later the same day. Wikipedia groups top, heart and base notes by the time since a perfume was applied, so a second check gives you a chance to notice the heart and base as well as the opening.

Keep to one pair a day. Write a single line about each fragrance using a note from its list, such as "I kept noticing the almond" or "the cinnamon stood out." Those three pairs cover every fragrance on the chart except Perseus, which you can try on a fourth day beside Layton or Haltane, the two lists it overlaps with most.

Frequently asked questions

Which Parfums de Marly fragrance should I try first?

Pick the one whose official notes include a smell you already like. Apple and lavender point to Parfums de Marly Layton; cinnamon and orange blossom over vanilla point to Althair; praline with leather points to Haltane; grapefruit and vetiver point to Perseus; almond and anise point to Pegasus. Bergamot is on all five lists, so it cannot settle the choice. This order follows the note lists and is not a quality ranking.

Is Layton or Pegasus the better first Parfums de Marly?

Neither is better; they are built from different notes. Parfums de Marly Layton and Pegasus share only bergamot and vanilla. Layton adds apple, cardamom, lavender, violet, geranium, patchouli, guaiacwood and praline. Pegasus adds anise, almond, cypress, violet leaves, orange blossom, ambrox and sandalwood. If the Layton notes are the ones you already enjoy, start there; if almond and anise sound more like you, start with Pegasus.

What is the difference between Althair and Layton?

Parfums de Marly Althair and Layton share five listed notes, bergamot, cardamom, vanilla, guaiacwood and praline, which is more than any other pair among these five. Althair adds mandarin, elemi, orange blossom and cinnamon, and names its vanilla bourbon vanilla. Layton adds apple, lavender, violet, geranium and patchouli. Cardamom also changes position: it is a top note in Layton and a heart note in Althair.

Which Parfums de Marly fragrances have vanilla in them?

Of these five Parfums de Marly fragrances, Layton and Pegasus list vanilla among their base notes, and Althair lists bourbon vanilla, a name for Vanilla planifolia taken from Île Bourbon, the former name of Réunion. Haltane and Perseus list no vanilla on their official pages. Layton, Althair and Haltane all list praline, a confection of nuts, usually almonds, pecans and hazelnuts, and sugar.

How is Perseus different from the other Parfums de Marly fragrances here?

By its note list. Parfums de Marly Perseus has seven notes, the shortest list of the five: bergamot and grapefruit on top, geranium and blackcurrant bud in the heart, vetiver, amberwood and grey amber in the base. It is the only one with grapefruit, blackcurrant bud, amberwood or grey amber, and it lists no vanilla, praline or lavender. The house describes "bright grapefruit, fresh geranium, and refined vetiver". It shares geranium with Layton and vetiver with Haltane.

Is Parfums de Marly a niche brand, and where are its perfumes made?

Wikipedia describes Parfums de Marly as a French niche perfume house, established in 2009 by Julien Sprecher and headquartered in Paris. The house's official product pages for Layton, Althair, Haltane, Perseus and Pegasus each state "All our perfumes are made in France". The brand's name is inspired by the Château de Marly, which Wikipedia describes as a residence of King Louis XV.
