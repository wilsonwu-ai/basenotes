What Bond No. 9 actually is

Bond No. 9 is an American luxury perfume house founded in 2003 by Laurice Rahmé, a Parisian-born perfumer who built her career in the United States. Rahmé’s background is more rigorous than most independent-niche founders: she spent years at Lancôme as a training director, then worked at L’Oréal USA as an artistic director, and is credited as the first woman to head a New York perfumery. The house takes its name from its original address: 9 Bond Street, in the NoHo neighborhood of Lower Manhattan.

The founding story the brand tells is worth taking seriously, because it informs the philosophy behind the catalog. Rahmé has said publicly that Bond No. 9 was born from her love for New York City as she watched it rebuild after September 11, 2001. That origin is not a marketing gloss — it explains why the neighborhood-naming concept carries the emotional weight it does for people who know the city. The fragrances are not named after neighborhoods as a branding shortcut. They are designed to evoke specific sensory memories associated with those places: the kind of thing that is either resonant or entirely abstract depending on whether you have walked those streets. Both readings work. The fragrances stand on their own without the backstory. But the backstory deepens them for the people who have the reference.

Bond No. 9 launched with 16 fragrances, all named after New York neighborhoods. The catalog has grown to over 100 releases in more than 20 years. The house has released fragrances named after neighborhoods most New Yorkers recognize instantly (Bleecker Street, TriBeCa, the Hamptons) and a handful of culturally specific references (Silver Factory is an Andy Warhol nod; Chelsea Nights is the gallery district after dark). The depth and specificity of the catalog is, depending on your perspective, either a sign of genuine creative ambition or a discovery problem for a new buyer. Both things are true, which is why this guide exists.

The bottle — design as a statement, not just packaging

One thing that distinguishes Bond No. 9 before you smell anything: the bottles. Each fragrance features the house’s distinctive star-point cap and a colorful, often bold enamel-inlaid label specific to that neighborhood. The bottles are genuinely collectible in a way that most luxury fragrance packaging is not — closer to art objects than to presentation boxes. This is part of what Rahmé built deliberately: a New York luxury object that functions as décor as well as fragrance. If the visual identity on a shelf matters to you, Bond No. 9 delivers it in a way that even Creed and Tom Ford do not at the same price point. If the bottle is irrelevant and you care only about what is inside, that is fine too — but the pricing includes it, and understanding that saves the confusion of comparing per-ml cost against houses that do not invest at the same level in the object.

A practical guide to entering the catalog

Five fragrances that cover the meaningful range of what Bond No. 9 does well, and which to try first depending on how you think about fragrance.

Bleecker Street — the accessible entry point

Bleecker Street is Bond No. 9’s most mainstream-facing fragrance and the logical starting point for anyone coming from designer fragrance for the first time. The notes are blueberry, violet, and vanilla — a gourmand that leans youthful without reading as a body spray. The blueberry is more of a juicy, slightly tart fruit note than a candy interpretation; the violet provides soft, slightly powdery structure; the vanilla base rounds everything into a smooth, approachable drydown. If you wear fragrances in the Acqua di Gio or Dior Sauvage family and want to understand what happens to a clean, crowd-pleasing aesthetic when serious perfumery resources are applied to it, Bleecker Street is the answer. It will not challenge you. It will consistently earn approachable, positive reactions. For someone new to Bond No. 9, that is precisely the right first experience.

New York Nights — the masculine, clean option

New York Nights is the Bond No. 9 fragrance most legible to someone coming from the mainstream masculine market. The character is crisp and aquatic with projection that registers without being aggressive — the community consistently describes it as earning compliments in offices and social settings without demanding that the people around you adjust to anything unfamiliar. It is not Bond No. 9 at its most creative or most distinctive. It is Bond No. 9 at its most immediately wearable for a broad male audience. Whether that sounds like a compromise depends on what you want from fragrance; if the goal is reliable, confident wear that does not ask anything difficult, New York Nights is exactly that.

Brooklyn — the spicy, structured option

Brooklyn was designed by Rahmé herself and launched in 2008. It is an oriental spicy fragrance intended for both men and women — denser and more compositionally assertive than Bleecker Street, designed for the wearer who has some experience with niche fragrance and wants something with genuine weight. If you wear Parfums de Marly Layton or Tom Ford Ombré Leather and want to understand what Bond No. 9 does with richer, more demanding material, Brooklyn is the relevant reference. It is not as immediately legible as Bleecker Street, and that is the point. Rahmé built this one for the wearer who already knows what they are doing and wants a fragrance that rewards that knowledge.

Chelsea Nights — the layered, premium entry

Chelsea Nights builds from rose, turmeric, agarwood, and patchouli — a combination with more compositional complexity than almost anything else in the mainstream Bond No. 9 catalog. The turmeric is not a background spice note in the way that black pepper is used in designer fragrances; it is earthy and slightly bitter, giving the rose something to push against rather than letting it float. The agarwood base provides resinous depth. This is Bond No. 9 for the wearer who has worked through the accessible catalog and wants to understand what the house can do with demanding materials. Not a fragrance for a first encounter with the house — but for someone who has been wearing Creed Aventus or Tom Ford for two years and wants to explore a different direction, Chelsea Nights is one of the most interesting things Bond No. 9 makes.

Greenwich Village — the unisex, versatile option

Greenwich Village is the catalog’s most genuinely cross-demographic fragrance: an oriental floral opening over a gourmand-woody base that the community consistently describes as working well across gender and across seasons. If you are buying a fragrance as a gift and do not know the recipient’s preferences with specificity, Greenwich Village is the Bond No. 9 fragrance most likely to land correctly. It is also the right starting point if your own fragrance wardrobe runs androgynous or unisex and you want to understand what Bond No. 9 does in that space without starting from one of the more obviously gendered entries.

The sampling problem — and why it matters more here than with most houses

Bond No. 9 full bottles run approximately $150–$175 for smaller presentations (typically 50 ml) and $250–$475 for larger bottles, depending on the specific fragrance and edition. Special releases and limited editions can run higher. At those price points, the conventional sampling process — a counter visit, a spritz on a test strip, a walk around the department store — is a genuinely inadequate way to make a $300 decision. We cover the math on why counter impressions are insufficient for full-bottle decisions in our piece on what fragrance subscribers actually switch to. The short version: skin chemistry, temperature, and time change the character of a fragrance in ways that a five-minute counter impression cannot capture. This matters especially for Bond No. 9, because the base notes are where the house’s quality investment is most visible — and the base is what you smell two or three hours after application, not thirty seconds after a counter spray.

A 5 ml atomizer provides approximately 35–50 days of real wear at two sprays per day. That is enough time to know whether a fragrance is genuinely yours before committing $300 to a bottle. It is also a meaningful fraction of the time you would spend with that full bottle in the first rotation year. The math on sampling at $20 for a month versus spending $300 without adequate testing is not complicated. The only variable is whether you value the testing period enough to build it into the process. Every serious fragrance buyer we know has done it this way at least once. Most of them do it every time now.

Is Bond No. 9 worth the price?

Yes, for the fragrances that suit you — and no for the ones that do not. That is a deliberately specific answer to what sounds like a general question, but it is the honest one. Bond No. 9’s catalog spans more than 100 releases across 20-plus years of operation. The accessible entries (Bleecker Street, New York Nights) offer real luxury ingredient quality at prices that compare reasonably to Tom Ford Signature or Parfums de Marly’s accessible tier. The premium catalog entries (Chelsea Nights, certain limited editions) reflect the kind of raw material investment that $350-plus pricing is defensible for. What is never defensible is spending that money on a fragrance you have not properly worn across multiple days and temperature conditions on your own skin. That is not a caveat specific to Bond No. 9. It is how fragrance buying works at this price point.

The house Rahmé built has genuine staying power — launching in 2003 and maintaining creative output and loyal buyers for more than two decades in the luxury fragrance category is not an accident. The design investment, the ingredient sourcing, and the creative coherence of the NYC concept are all real. Frequently asked questions

Where is Bond No. 9 sold?

Bond No. 9 sells through its flagship stores in New York City and on its official website at bondno9.com. The fragrances are also available at select luxury department stores (Neiman Marcus, Nordstrom, Saks) and authorized specialty fragrance retailers. Authorized retailers maintain consistent pricing; below-retail pricing on secondary markets may indicate older stock or improperly stored bottles, which affects fragrance quality over time.

Is Bond No. 9 a niche or designer house?

Bond No. 9 sits in what the fragrance community calls the “luxury niche” category: independently owned, with a dedicated founder and coherent creative vision, but with distribution and name recognition significantly broader than most boutique artisan houses. It is not mass-distributed the way Chanel, Dior, or Tom Ford Signature is. It is not as boutique as the smallest independent houses. It occupies a meaningful middle tier, which is reflected in the pricing. The Fragrantica community pages for Bond No. 9 carry extensive per-fragrance reviews from enthusiasts if you want to go deeper on any specific scent before sampling.

How does Bond No. 9 compare to Creed or Tom Ford?

All three are serious luxury fragrance houses with genuinely different identities. Creed is European heritage, a catalog that leans toward classic, refined structures, and a buyer base that values lineage and tradition. Tom Ford divides into two tiers — Signature (accessible luxury, $150–$250) and Private Blend (niche luxury, $250–$600-plus) — with a strongly fashion-forward, sensual identity across both. Bond No. 9 is unapologetically American and specifically New York: more playful, more design-forward, and more narrative-driven than either. Which one is right depends on what you want fragrance to feel like, not on which house is objectively better. All three are in our catalog, and the most useful thing we can offer is a month of real wear on each — because reading about them is not the same as wearing them.

What Bond No. 9 fragrance makes the best gift?

If you know the recipient’s preferences: use the guide above to match. If you are buying without knowing what they wear: Greenwich Village is the safest first choice for its cross-demographic wearability. Bleecker Street works well if you know the recipient’s preferences run sweet and accessible. For a fragrance enthusiast who already wears luxury niche: Brooklyn or Chelsea Nights, if you have enough knowledge of what they currently wear to know that complexity and depth are what they want. A Bond No. 9 gift is best received when the specific fragrance was chosen with some knowledge of the person — the NYC concept is distinctive enough that a thoughtfully chosen neighborhood carries real meaning for the right recipient.
