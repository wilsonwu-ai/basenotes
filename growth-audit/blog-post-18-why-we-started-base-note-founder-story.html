The $500 Mistake, Repeated

If you’ve spent real money on luxury fragrance, you’ve probably made some version of this mistake. You spray something at a department store counter, or on a friend’s wrist at a dinner party, or on a paper blotter handed to you by someone whose job is to close the sale — and you love it. The salesperson wraps the box before you’ve fully thought it through. And $350–$500 later, you own a fragrance you’ve committed to without actually knowing whether it will become part of your life.

For us, that mistake was not one bottle. It was several. Creed Aventus at $395 for 100 ml. Parfums de Marly Layton at $290 for 75 ml. A couple of Tom Ford releases in the $300–$450 range. The kind of fragrances that earn a crowd in a room and justify a lengthy first impression at a counter. Some of them were right — a bottle you wear every other day for three years is obviously worth $400. But several were not right. Several sat on a shelf, used twice, while we reached for the same three scents we actually trusted and told ourselves we’d “get back to” the expensive disappointment at some point.

The moment the problem clarified itself: we realized our actual fragrance behavior was a rotation. Not a signature scent. Not the one-bottle life the fragrance industry’s advertising had sold us. A rotation — maybe four or five scents, worn for different occasions, different seasons, different moods. Something lighter for work. Something that projects louder for a night out. Something warm and ambery for cold weather, something crisper when the heat hit. None of them worn enough to justify a full 100 ml bottle at luxury-house pricing. All of them bought as full bottles anyway because that was the only real option.

The math on being a rotation person who buys full bottles: you are paying $1,200–$2,000 a year for a category of experience you could theoretically have for a fraction of that, if there were a service built for your actual behavior rather than the behavior the industry assumes you have.

There wasn’t. Not quite. Not for the specific combination of things we wanted.

How Our Fragrance Habits Actually Worked

There is a version of fragrance ownership the industry sells: you find your signature scent, you wear it every day, it becomes part of your identity. That version is real. It probably describes the majority of casual fragrance wearers, and there is nothing wrong with it. One great fragrance worn confidently is better than twelve mediocre ones worn anxiously.

It did not describe us. We were interested in fragrance as an ongoing interest, not as a single committed purchase. We read Fragrantica threads the way other people read wine reviews — curious about what specific notes would do on specific skin types, interested in the houses and the perfumers behind them, wanting to experience a wider range than one bottle per year allows. We wore Creed for a season, then wanted to understand what Parfums de Marly Layton’s vanilla-amber drydown actually did at our skin temperature before we committed to it. We had been hearing about Xerjoff Erba Pura for years and wanted to live with it for a month before deciding if it was worth the bottle price.

This is a completely legitimate way to approach fragrance. It is also the way of approaching fragrance that existing services were worst suited to support.

What We Tried Before Starting Base Note

We were not the first people to notice this problem, and before we built anything, we spent real time and real money trying what existed. Here is the honest ledger:

À-la-carte sample sites and decant communities. Sites that sell individual 1–2 ml samples — and the decant communities on fragrance forums — are genuinely excellent for first impressions. We used them, and we still point people toward them for one-off curiosity. The limitation is practical: a 1–2 ml spray does not tell you how a fragrance performs on your skin across real conditions. Fragrance changes over hours and over weeks as your skin chemistry interacts with it. A first impression on a blotter or from a single spray is a teaser, not a verdict. We once tried Parfums de Marly Herod on a sample, thought it wasn’t for us, tried it again three months later in winter, and decided it was one of the better fragrances we’d ever worn. A 1 ml sample would not have given us that corrected read.

The mainstream fragrance subscription model. The leading services existed before we started Base Note, and we had both subscribed to them at various points. The problem was not the concept — monthly vials, pick your own scent, cancel anytime. The problem was the execution for our specific use case. If what you want is Creed Aventus at a flat monthly price, Scentbird’s headline rate is not your actual price. The premium surcharge for luxury and niche brands — often $5–$15 extra on top of the subscription fee, applied to the exact releases you signed up to try — means the math does not work the way the marketing suggests. We have covered this in more depth in our guide to Scentbird alternatives and in our full fragrance-subscription comparison. The short version: the category existed, but it was built primarily around designer-mass catalogs at entry-price points, with luxury as an upsell — not luxury as the default, at one price.

Department-store counters with more discipline. For a while we tried simply being more rigorous about the in-store process: spray, wait for the full drydown, come back the next day, ask for samples. This works in theory. In practice, most fragrance counters do not give useful samples of luxury releases. Brand sample allocation is controlled at the house level, and for a house like Creed, which sells primarily through its own boutiques and a handful of luxury department stores, getting your hands on a proper take-home sample of a specific release you want to evaluate is genuinely difficult. We once spent three weeks tracking down an Aventus sample before giving up and buying the bottle. The counter model has structural limits that are not going to change, because the houses that make the fragrances we wanted have no incentive to make sampling frictionless.

None of these solutions were wrong. All of them were incomplete.

What We Actually Wanted (and Could Not Find)

The product we wanted was simple to describe:

  • A 5 ml vial — large enough to actually wear for a month and form a real opinion, not just sniff once and move on
  • Of the actual luxury and niche fragrances we wanted to try: Creed, Tom Ford, Parfums de Marly, Xerjoff, Bond No. 9 — not a catalog padded with designer-mass releases with a few luxury names available at a surcharge
  • At one flat price, with no surcharge game and no premium tier
  • With the option to pick which scent we received each month, because the whole premise was that we were building a rotation intelligently — not being surprised with a curator’s pick
  • That canceled cleanly, without friction, if it stopped being useful

That product did not exist. We built it.

How We Built It — and What We Got Wrong

We are not going to write a founder story that skips the mistakes, because the mistakes are where the interesting stuff lives.

We underestimated sourcing complexity. Building a curated catalog of genuine luxury fragrances — from houses like Creed, which sells primarily through its own boutiques, and Parfums de Marly, which runs tightly controlled US distribution — at the unit economics that allow a $20 flat subscription to stay sustainable, is not a straightforward supply-chain problem. It took longer than we projected. We made some early sourcing decisions that we revised before launch. We are being deliberately vague here, not to obscure the difficulty, but because the details are operational and boring. The point is: it was harder than it looked, and we figured it out.

We initially thought the catalog needed to be large. Early planning included a much longer list of available fragrances, on the theory that breadth signals value. What we found was that subscribers come to us with four or five specific scents they want to try, and the quality and curation of what’s in the catalog matters more than the count. We narrowed to a tighter list of genuinely exceptional releases. Some subscribers want 800 options; those subscribers are probably better served by services with 800-scent catalogs. We are not trying to be that, and we will say so clearly rather than pretend to compete on dimension we are not competing on.

We debated the price point for a long time. $20 is higher than some competitors’ headline rates. It is often lower than what those services actually charge on a luxury month, once surcharges are applied. The honest tradeoff: if you primarily want mass-designer fragrances — Armani, Ralph Lauren, Versace — and you are not interested in the Creed or Parfums de Marly tier, Base Note is probably not for you, and a cheaper service makes more sense. We said this in our comparison article when we launched and we still mean it. The flat-price model only makes sense if you are actually going to use the catalog for what it is.

What Base Note Is Today

The service is intentionally simple: $20 per month, one 5 ml travel atomizer, you pick the scent from the catalog, ships free in the US. The catalog is the houses we care about — the ones that charge $250–$500 for a full bottle because the fragrance inside genuinely justifies it. No surcharges, no premium tiers, no “luxury add-on.” If it is in the catalog, it is $20. That simplicity was not an accident. It was a deliberate decision to build the service we wanted to exist rather than the service that was easiest to monetize.

The positioning we settled on — one flat price, every luxury fragrance, build your signature rotation — is not marketing copy we workshopped to death. It is a literal description of the problem we had and what we built to solve it.

We are two people who love fragrance, who kept spending money on full bottles we weren’t sure about, and who built the service that would have saved us that money. If you are that person too, we think this is for you. If you are not that person — if you wear one fragrance consistently and a full bottle is obviously the right move — we would genuinely rather tell you that than take your $20 for something you don’t need.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why 5 ml and not 8 ml like other services?

A 5 ml travel atomizer at two sprays per day gives you 25–37 wearing days — roughly one full month of daily wear. That is enough time to form a genuine opinion about whether a fragrance belongs in your permanent rotation. Skin chemistry, seasonal conditions, and real-world context (office, weekend, evening) all shape how a fragrance reads on you, and it takes actual weeks of wear to understand that. An 8 ml vial at the same usage rate gives you two months, which sounds better but extends your evaluation period for a scent you might conclude is wrong for you in week three. We chose the size that matches a monthly cadence for a reason.

What if I already own some of these fragrances?

Then the catalog is most useful to you for the titles you haven’t tried yet — the ones you have been curious about but haven’t committed to at full-bottle pricing. Some subscribers use a month or two to evaluate a new scent, love it, buy the full bottle, and continue subscribing to keep exploring. Others use it as a long-term rotation tool and never need to buy full bottles because a monthly 5 ml actually covers their real usage. Both are fine. We are not trying to talk you out of full-bottle purchases if you find something you love — the whole point is helping you find that thing without the expensive mistakes along the way.

Is the fragrance in your atomizers authentic?

Yes. Everything in the catalog is authentic. This is non-negotiable for us — the entire premise of the service depends on subscribers being able to evaluate whether they want to buy a full bottle based on what they receive from us. If what we sent was not the real fragrance, that evaluation is worthless. The fragrance community is also expert at detecting counterfeits, and our reputation is the most important thing we own. The authenticity question is one we take seriously and that we will never give anyone reason to ask twice.

What does cancellation actually look like?

Your subscription ends at the close of the current billing period. No friction, no “are you sure” dark patterns, no win-back flows designed to wear you down. If it stops being useful to you, cancel. If you want to come back, the catalog will still be there. We built this to be worth subscribing to, not to be difficult to leave. The services with F-rated cancellation experiences are part of why we exist — we are not going to replicate the behavior that drove people to look for alternatives.

Do you plan to expand the catalog?

Selectively. We add fragrances when we can source them at terms that allow the flat price to hold without cutting quality or authenticity. We will not add to the catalog for the sake of a larger number on a marketing page. Every addition is evaluated the same way we evaluate any purchase: would we wear it, does it belong in the tier we operate in, and can we stand behind it with our own names on the recommendation.

The Bottom Line

We started Base Note because we were spending too much money on wrong full bottles and none of the existing services solved the specific problem we had. We wanted the luxury houses — the ones that charge $290–$500 for a reason, made by perfumers with real craft behind the price tag — at a flat monthly price that made building a fragrance rotation financially sensible rather than financially reckless.

If that is your problem too, we think we solved it. If you are still orienting on where Base Note sits relative to the other services in the category, our honest fragrance-subscription comparison names where we lose to competitors as clearly as where we win.

And if that is not your problem — if you have your scent, and a full bottle is obviously the right move, and a subscription is an unnecessary complication — we are glad you found yours. That is the actual goal. We just wanted a way to find ours without the expensive wrong turns.
