Projection, sillage, longevity: three different things people keep merging

Almost every argument about office fragrance is really an argument about one of these three, and people rarely say which.

Projection is the radius of the scent bubble around you right now. High projection means someone standing four feet away smells you without leaning in. This is the one that gets you talked about at work, because an open-plan desk is about four feet from the next open-plan desk.

Sillage (French, roughly “wake”) is the trail left behind after you have moved. High sillage means people know you walked down the corridor a minute after you did. This is the one that gets you talked about in elevators and small conference rooms, where the air does not turn over.

Longevity is simply how many hours the fragrance is still detectable on your own skin. It is independent of the other two.

Here is the part that reframes the whole problem: the ideal office fragrance has high longevity and low projection. Those are not in conflict. A scent can sit close to the skin for nine hours. What most people do instead is buy something with modest longevity, notice it has faded by 11 a.m., and compensate by spraying more in the morning — which raises projection for the first two hours without meaningfully extending the tail. The complaint arrives at 9:15, and by the time you personally stop smelling it around lunch, you conclude the fragrance is weak and spray more tomorrow.

Your own nose is the unreliable instrument here. Olfactory adaptation means you stop registering your own fragrance long before other people do. That gap is the single largest cause of over-application, and it is also why fragrance behaves differently on different people — skin chemistry, oil levels and what you ate all shift the dry-down. The complaint statistic, read honestly

You will see a number quoted in nearly every office-fragrance article: roughly 35% of people report problems with fragrance. It comes from a real, well-conducted study, and it does not mean what the headlines say.

The source is Anne Steinemann’s 2016 paper in Air Quality, Atmosphere & Health, based on a national random sample of 1,136 American adults, representative of age, gender and region. The finding: “Overall, 34.7 % of the population reported health problems, such as migraine headaches and respiratory difficulties, when exposed to fragranced products.” (Source: Steinemann, Air Quality, Atmosphere & Health, 2016)

Read the definition, though. The paper defines “fragranced consumer products” as “chemically formulated consumer products with a fragrance,” and the examples it gives are “air fresheners, cleaning supplies, soaps, hand sanitizers, laundry supplies, and personal care products.” That is the plug-in in the office restroom, the industrial cleaner used overnight, and your colleague’s laundry detergent — all bundled together with fine fragrance. Nobody has published a clean number for “how many people are bothered specifically by a coworker’s cologne,” and anyone who tells you 35% of your coworkers hate your fragrance is over-reading the data.

Two other findings from the same paper are more directly relevant to how you should behave at work, and both are worth taking seriously:

  • “Further, 15.1 % have lost workdays or a job due to fragranced product exposure in the workplace.”
  • “Of the population surveyed, 53.2 % would be supportive of a fragrance-free policy in the workplace (compared to 19.7 % that would not).”

So: the 35% headline is softer than advertised, but the underlying point stands. A meaningful share of people are genuinely affected, a majority would vote for a fragrance-free office, and some of those people are sitting near you. If you have a coworker who has told you a fragrance triggers migraines or asthma, that is a medical issue and not a taste debate — take it at face value, wear nothing near them, and if you are the one having reactions, talk to a doctor rather than diagnosing it from a blog post.

The eight picks, and why each one works

1. Parfums de Marly Perseus — the default answer

Grapefruit, bergamot and green apple up top; lavender, geranium and violet leaf in the heart; musk, amber and woody notes underneath. It is the most straightforwardly correct office fragrance in our rotation: bright enough to register as deliberate, structured so the loud citrus burns off in half an hour and leaves a clean musk that stays close. If you want to stop thinking about this, wear Perseus.

2. Rabanne Mesh Metal — the minimalist

An unusually short note list: lime and petitgrain bigarade, orange blossom, ambergris. Short pyramids tend to behave predictably, and this one hugs the skin. It reads as expensive soap in the best sense. Of the eight, this is the one we would recommend to somebody who has previously been told they wear too much.

3. Bond No. 9 FiDi — named for a trading floor, and it shows

Black pepper, bergamot and mandarin, into lotus and nutmeg, landing on musk, cedar and a whisper of vanilla. The pepper gives it some spine so it does not read as generic fresh, and the cedar-musk base is quiet. Works year-round, which most of this list does not.

4. Xerjoff Torino 21 — the one for hot commutes

Mint, lemon, basil and thyme; black currant, lavender, rosemary and jasmine; lemon verbena and musk. Genuinely herbal rather than sweet-fresh. The honest caveat is longevity: this is a bright, volatile composition, and you may want to reapply after lunch. That is a fair trade for a July commute. More on hot-weather picks in our summer fragrance guide.

5. Creed Green Irish Tweed — the classic, with an asterisk

French verbena and lemon verbena, Florentine iris and violet leaves, Mysore sandalwood and ambergris. It has been the reference “clean and expensive” masculine for decades and the sandalwood base is beautiful. The asterisk: this is not a quiet fragrance. It projects more than anything else on this list, so treat one spray as the default and two as the ceiling. If you like it and want to know how it compares to its stablemate, that is a rabbit hole for another day.

6. Prada Paradigme — the cold-weather office pick

Calabrian bergamot and airy musks, green bourbon geranium and rosy facets, then Peru balm, benzoin and guaiac wood. Most office lists are summer lists, and then October arrives and citrus feels thin. Paradigme is the answer: woody and slightly warm without tipping into the sweet gourmand territory that gets noticed in a heated conference room. Pairs naturally with the rest of our fall picks.

7. Bond No. 9 The Scent of Peace for Him — the crowd-pleaser

Pineapple, juniper berries and bergamot over currant buds, with cedar and vetiver at the base. The most immediately likeable thing here, and the one most likely to draw a compliment. It is also the strongest performer of the fresh group, so it earns the lowest spray count on the table. One spray is genuinely enough.

8. Mont Blanc Legend Eau de Parfum — the safe year-rounder

Bergamot, grapefruit and lavender; amber, cedarwood and tonka bean; sandalwood, musk and vetiver. The tonka and amber make it the sweetest thing on this list, which is why it is last. In a cool office it is a comfortable, competent, slightly cozy woody. In a warm one, sweetness amplifies — if your building runs hot, move down the list.

The borderline case, and what to leave at home

YSL Y Intense gets tagged as office-appropriate constantly, and we tag it that way too. We think that is optimistic. Ginger, bergamot and juniper open cleanly, but vetiver, patchouli and cedar in the base give it real density and a long tail. It is a great fragrance and a fine choice for a client dinner. For a Tuesday at a desk, it is doing more work than the room asked for.

From our own rotation, the ones we would actively steer away from a normal office: Parfums de Marly Layton and Parfums de Marly Althair (both vanilla-forward and built to carry), Viktor & Rolf Spicebomb Extreme (the name is not a metaphor), Initio Side Effect, and Xerjoff Alexandria II. None of these are mistakes. They are evening fragrances, and the rooms they are built for have more air in them.

Why a rotation solves this better than a bottle does

The structural problem with office fragrance is that the correct answer changes. A February conference room and an August commute want different things, and a client dinner wants a third. Buying a full bottle commits you to roughly 250 wearings of one decision, which is why so many people end up wearing an evening fragrance to work — it is the bottle they own.
