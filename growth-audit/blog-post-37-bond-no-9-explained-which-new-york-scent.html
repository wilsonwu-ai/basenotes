If you only read one thing: Bond No. 9 names its fragrances after New York neighborhoods, but the name tells you nothing about the smell — the note list does. Of the four we carry, the split is clean. The Scent of Peace for Him is the hot-weather, office-safe, pineapple-and-vetiver one. FiDi is the year-round versatile one, peppery-citrus into soft woods. Bleecker Street is the sweet one — blueberry, caramel, suede — and it is the one people either love or find too dessert-adjacent. NoMad is the cold-weather evening statement, built on an oud accord with rose and amber. Buy on the notes, not the neighborhood.

What Bond No. 9 actually is

Bond No. 9 was founded in 2003 by Laurice Rahmé, with its main shop at 9 Bond Street in the NoHo district of Manhattan — which is where the name comes from. The house opened with 16 “neighborhood fragrances,” and by 2013 had produced “over 60 fragrances, including exclusive co-branded fragrance productions for Saks Fifth Avenue and Harrods” (Source: Wikipedia, Bond No. 9, retrieved 2026-08-18). The same entry notes five eponymous New York boutiques plus distribution through Saks, Harrods, Bloomingdale’s, Hudson’s Bay, Isetan, Sephora and De Bijenkorf, and two FiFi Award wins — Astor Place and Brooklyn taking Fragrance of the Year in the women’s and men’s categories in 2009, and New York Oud taking Perfume Extraordinaire in 2012.

The neighborhood conceit is the whole brand identity, and it is worth being clear-eyed about it. A neighborhood name is a brief given to a perfumer, not a description handed to a shopper. Nothing about the Financial District smells like black pepper and mandarin. NoMad is a good example of how much the name can mislead: our own product page describes it in terms of wanderlust and travel, but the house means NoMad the district — North of Madison Square Park — which Bond No. 9 describes as “one of the city’s oldest districts, which has continuously reinvented itself over generations” (Source: Bond No. 9, checked 2026-08-18). Both readings are fine as poetry. Neither tells you the fragrance is an oud.

So: read the pyramid, ignore the geography. If pyramids are new to you, our guide to top, heart and base notes covers how to read one bottom-up, and our fragrance families explainer covers the category vocabulary.

The four, one at a time

The Scent of Peace for Him — the easy one

Notes on our page: pineapple, juniper berries and bergamot up top; currant buds in the heart; cedar and vetiver in the base.

Pineapple is the headline, and it is worth saying that this is not a tropical-drink pineapple. Juniper and vetiver pull it dry almost immediately, so the effect is closer to crisp-and-green than sweet. It reads clean, it reads fresh, and it does not demand anything of the room.

Who it suits: anyone who wants a warm-weather fragrance that will not become a topic of conversation. If you work in a shared office, this is the one of the four we would hand you first — the same logic our office-safe colognes guide runs through in more detail.

The honest caveat: fruity-fresh openings with a vetiver base are a crowded category, and this one does not do anything structurally surprising. You are paying Bond No. 9 prices for a very well-executed version of a familiar idea. That is a fair trade for some people and an obvious no for others.

FiDi — the versatile one

Notes on our page: black pepper, bergamot and mandarin orange up top; lotus and nutmeg in the heart; musk, cedar and vanilla in the base.

Black pepper over citrus is a sharp, adult opening — it announces itself without being loud. The nutmeg keeps a little spice alive through the middle, and then it settles into soft, slightly powdery woods with just enough vanilla to keep it from going austere. Our page tags it for spring, summer and fall, which is unusual across our catalogue and is the single best argument for it.

Who it suits: the person who wants one fragrance to cover most of the calendar. If you are building a rotation from scratch, a versatile spicy-woody like this is a strong middle slot.

The honest caveat: note lists for FiDi differ depending on where you look. Our page lists a base of musk, cedar and vanilla; Bond No. 9’s own page for the same fragrance also lists tonka bean, ambrox and amber in the base (Source: Bond No. 9, checked 2026-08-18). Neither list is wrong — published pyramids are editorial summaries, not formulas — but it is a useful reminder that a note list is a map, not the territory.

Bleecker Street — the divisive one

Notes on our page: violet leaf, blueberry, cassis and thyme up top; cedar, jasmine and cinnamon in the heart; oakmoss, suede, patchouli, amber, vanilla and caramel in the base.

That base is the story. Six materials, and three of them — amber, vanilla, caramel — are sweet. Blueberry and cassis up front make the opening read almost like a dessert, and then oakmoss, suede and patchouli arrive underneath and drag it somewhere drier and older-feeling. Bond No. 9 files it under warm and spicy, as a woody gourmand oriental.

Who it suits: people who already know they like sweet fragrance and want a version with actual structure under it. The mossy-suede floor is what separates this from a straightforward gourmand.

The honest caveat: this is the one of the four most likely to be too much for someone. If “caramel” in a base note list makes you wince, believe that instinct. It is also the one where the seasonal tagging on our own page — spring and summer — sits oddly with how sweet and heavy the dry-down is; we would reach for it in cooler months, and our fall fragrance guide explains why sweetness and cold air work well together.

NoMad — the evening one

Notes on our page: pear, quince and blackcurrant up top; rose, oud accord, violet leaves and orris in the heart; amber, vanilla and sandalwood in the base.

Fruit into oud into amber is a well-established formula for a reason: the pear and quince give you a bright, almost juicy first two minutes, and then the rose-and-oud heart takes over and the whole thing gets serious. The house calls it “a strong, sensual, statement-making oud, with modern fruity notes and luxurious amber” (Source: Bond No. 9, checked 2026-08-18), which is a fair description.

Who it suits: evenings, cold weather, and occasions where you want the fragrance to be part of the outfit. Tagged on our page for date night, evenings out and formal wear — see also our date night cologne guide.

The honest caveat: oud is not neutral territory. Wear this to a 9am meeting and you will be the most noticeable person in the room, and not in the way you intended.

Where Bond No. 9 genuinely disappoints

Three honest criticisms, none of which we would expect the house to print.

The price is the product for some buyers. At $4.60–$4.70 per millilitre, you are paying for heavy decorated bottles, five Manhattan boutiques and a strong brand identity alongside the juice. Whether that is worth it is a personal call, but it should be a conscious one. Our piece on designer versus niche fragrance unpacks what that premium usually buys and what it does not.

The names encourage buying blind. “Which neighborhood are you?” is a fun question and a bad shopping method. Someone who loves the West Village and hates sweet fragrance will not enjoy Bleecker Street, and there is no version of the neighborhood story that warns them.

Published note lists are inconsistent. As above with FiDi, the same fragrance is described with materially different base notes on different pages. Treat every pyramid, including ours, as a summary written by a person rather than a specification.

How to actually test these

Spray on skin, not paper, and wait 30 minutes before forming an opinion. That is not a stylistic preference — the interaction between a person’s own body odor and a fragrance is measurable. In a controlled study where men wore their own chosen perfume on one armpit and a randomly assigned, equally pleasant perfume on the other, female raters found the self-selected blends significantly more attractive (Z = 2.37; N = 21; p = 0.02) and more pleasant (p = 0.01) (Source: PLOS ONE, 2012). The fragrance that suits you is partly a fact about your skin, not just your taste. Our pillar piece on why you smell the way you do goes deep on the mechanism, and our guide to sampling before you commit covers the testing routine itself.
