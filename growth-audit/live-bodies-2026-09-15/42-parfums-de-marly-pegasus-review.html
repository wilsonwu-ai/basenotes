If you only read one thing: The almond in Pegasus is real, but the shorthand is misleading. This is not a dessert. It is an almond-and-anise opening sitting on a heart of cypress, violet leaves and orange blossom, and that heart is what keeps the whole thing from reading as marzipan. What you actually get is closer to almond cream over a crisp, green-floral edge, drying down into vanilla, sandalwood and ambrox. If you love the almond-and-vanilla idea but hate anything that smells like a bakery, Pegasus is probably the one you have been looking for. If you want a straight gourmand with no green, floral spine, you want Althair instead. Test it on skin for at least 30 minutes before you decide — on a paper strip you will only ever smell the almond and anise, which is the part that misleads people.

What is actually in the bottle

Here is the note pyramid as it appears on our own product page for Parfums de Marly Pegasus, an Eau de Parfum made in France.

Tier Notes What it does in the wear

Two things stand out if you read that pyramid bottom-up, the way we argue for in our guide to top, heart and base notes. The base is woody, not gourmand. The heart is a cypress-and-floral heart. Both are invisible in the phrase “almond vanilla,” and both are why people expecting a scented candle come away confused.

So is the almond-vanilla hype real?

Mostly, with one correction and one caveat.

The correction: almond is doing less of the work than the hype suggests. Anise is the quieter half of that accord — a cool, faintly liquorice-adjacent note that sits under the almond rather than leading it. Paired together, the two blur, which is why the almond in Pegasus feels dimensional instead of sharp and synthetic. People say “almond” because that is the word available to them. What they are usually responding to is the almond-plus-anise blur.

The caveat: the cypress heart is not a garnish. It is roughly half the personality of this fragrance from about twenty minutes in. If you already know that cypress and green florals read as sharp or “aftershave” on you, Pegasus will inherit that, almond or no almond. This is the single most common reason we hear from people who tried Pegasus on a recommendation and did not click with it. It was never the almond. It was the cypress.

Our honest read: the hype is real for a specific person — someone who wants a sweet-adjacent fragrance that still smells groomed and does not want to smell like food. Pegasus threads that needle better than almost anything else in our rotation. The hype is not real if you were told it is a gourmand. We tag it floral, gourmand, vanilla and woody, and in wear the gourmand share is the smallest of the four.

Who Pegasus is for

  • People who like sweet but not sugary. If gourmands usually tip over into cloying on you, the cypress and sandalwood here act as brakes.
  • People who want one fragrance to cover date night and dinner with the in-laws. It is warm without being explicit about it.
  • Anyone building a cold-weather slot. We tag it fall and winter, and that is honest — the almond-vanilla-ambrox core gets heavy and slightly syrupy in heat. It belongs in the same seasonal slot we describe in our fall fragrance guide.
  • Couples who share. We tag Pegasus both masculine and unisex, and of the five Parfums de Marly bottles we carry it is the one most often worn by both people in a household.

Who should skip it

  • Anyone who dislikes cypress or green florals. Covered above. It is not negotiable in this composition.
  • Anyone shopping for an office scent. We tag it for date night, not for the office, and we mean it. For that slot see Perseus.
  • Anyone chasing distinctiveness. Pegasus is popular. It is a very good version of a familiar idea, not an unfamiliar one.
  • Warm-weather wearers. Almond, vanilla and ambrox all amplify in heat. Fewer sprays help; a different bottle helps more.

Pegasus vs. Layton

This is the real decision for most people, because Layton is the house's best-known bottle. Both open on bergamot and settle into vanilla — they share enough of a spine to count as siblings. The difference is what sits on top. Layton opens on apple, bergamot and cardamom and moves through a lavender, violet and geranium heart into patchouli and guaiacwood: fruitier, spicier, more floral. Pegasus opens on almond and anise and keeps the middle simple: nuttier, creamier, calmer. Layton is the more complex fragrance. Pegasus is the more comfortable one. If you want the long version on the other bottle, we published a full Layton review in September.

Pegasus vs. Althair

Not close, once you have smelled both. Althair is orange blossom, cinnamon, praline, vanilla and guaiacwood — a true gourmand with nothing pulling against the sweetness. Pegasus has cypress and sandalwood doing exactly that job. If someone tells you Pegasus is too sweet, they would find Althair unwearable. If someone tells you Pegasus is not sweet enough, Althair is the answer and you should stop shopping.

Pegasus vs. Haltane

Haltane is the house's dark one: bergamot and pink peppercorn up top, then saffron, praline, lavender and clary sage, landing on oud, patchouli, vetiver and leather. It shares a nutty, gourmand sweetness with Pegasus (praline there, almond here) and then buries it under smoke. Think of Pegasus as the version you wear to meet someone's parents and Haltane as the version you wear at 11 p.m. in a bar you chose deliberately. Haltane is also the harder wear of the two — leather and oud ask more of the wearer.

Pegasus vs. Perseus

These barely compete; they occupy opposite slots in a wardrobe. Perseus is bergamot and grapefruit over geranium and blackcurrant bud, finishing on vetiver, amberwood and grey amber — spring and summer, daytime and office. Most people who own both do not choose between them; they run Pegasus from October and Perseus from April. That two-bottle split is the simplest version of the approach in how to build a fragrance wardrobe.

Test it properly, because the reviews will not settle it for you

Reviews of Pegasus disagree more than reviews of most fragrances, and there is a decent scientific reason for that. In a set of controlled experiments, researchers found that the interaction between an individual wearer and the perfume applied to them was, in their words, “highly significant for all rated variables” — pleasantness, attractiveness and intensity. In the same work, when men wore a perfume they had chosen themselves on one armpit and a randomly assigned perfume of comparable general pleasantness on the other, raters judged the self-chosen blend significantly more attractive and more pleasant (Source: PLOS ONE, 2012). The practical translation: a fragrance is not a fixed object with a fixed verdict. It is a blend with your own skin, and the person telling you Pegasus is a masterpiece is reporting on a different blend than the one you would be wearing. We go deeper into that in our pillar piece on why you smell the way you do.

So: spray it on skin, not a card, and leave it alone for 30 minutes. The almond is a top note. Judging Pegasus in the first five minutes is judging the least representative part of it. A 5 ml atomizer holds roughly 60 sprays, which is enough for two to three weeks of real wear — morning, evening, work, a night out, one cold day, one mild one. That is the sample size a fragrance decision actually needs, and it is the whole argument for testing before you commit to a full bottle. We ran the arithmetic in how long a 5 ml atomizer lasts.

Frequently asked questions

Is Pegasus a gourmand?

Partly. We tag it floral, gourmand, vanilla and woody, and in wear the woody and floral sides are more prominent than the word “gourmand” implies. If you want an unambiguous gourmand from the same house, that is Althair. If you want to understand why these labels overlap so much, our guide to fragrance families covers it.

Is Pegasus unisex?

Yes, in our experience and in our own tagging. Almond, anise, vanilla and sandalwood are not gendered materials. The house markets much of its line to men; the bottle does not know that.

How long does it last?

We are not going to publish an hours figure, because longevity is one of the things that varies most from person to person and any single number would be dishonest precision. Concentration is the part that is standardised: Eau de Parfum sits at roughly 15–20% fragrance oil, against 5–15% for Eau de Toilette and 20–30% for parfum (Source: Ulta Beauty). Pegasus is an Eau de Parfum, and every product page on our site lists concentration and country of origin so you are never guessing. For what the terms actually mean, see projection vs. sillage vs. longevity.

How many sprays should I use?

Fewer than you think, especially indoors and especially in warm weather — almond, vanilla and ambrox all get louder as skin warms. Start at two, and add one only if nobody has noticed by lunchtime. Our piece on how many sprays of cologne to use goes through it properly.

Which Parfums de Marly should I try first?

If you have never worn the house: Layton, because it is the reference point everything else gets compared to. If you already know you like almond, marzipan or anise: Pegasus, directly. If you are not sure you like the house at all, run them in consecutive months — one 5 ml at a time, $20 each. That is what a rotation is for. The bottom line

Pegasus deserves most of its reputation and almost none of its shorthand. Called an almond-vanilla fragrance, it sounds like a dessert and disappoints people who wanted one. Described accurately — almond and anise, blurred creamy, laid over a cypress and orange blossom heart and finished with sandalwood and ambrox — it becomes obvious who it is for: someone who wants warmth and polish in the same spray, in cold weather, in the evening.

The honest caution is the cypress, not the sweetness. The honest recommendation is to test it on your own skin for half an hour before you take anybody's word for it, ours included. And the honest disclosure is that we would like that test to happen in one of our atomizers — $20, 5 ml, cancel anytime, and if it turns out Pegasus is not you, the next fragrance is next month and costs the same.
