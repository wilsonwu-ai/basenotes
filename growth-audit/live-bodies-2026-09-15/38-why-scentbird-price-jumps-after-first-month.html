Two things in that table should stop you. Scentbird’s catalogue is dramatically larger than ours and their standard vial is bigger than ours. We are not going to pretend otherwise. What we are arguing is narrower: that the shape of the price — what it does in month two, and what it does when you want something expensive — is the part most people get wrong when they sign up.

Mechanism one: the intro price is a first-month price

This is stated openly. The homepage banner on 18 August 2026 reads “Get 50% off your first month of Scentbird”, and the hero block reads “TRY ONE 8ML VIAL FOR $8.97 $17.95” with the $17.95 struck through (Source: Scentbird, 2026). The renewal price is not hidden. It is simply smaller, greyer and crossed out, and a shopper in a hurry reads the $8.97 as “the price” rather than as “the first invoice.”

The terms of service are more explicit about what that means for the second charge:

“Subscription renewals will be billed at the then-current rate of the applicable subscription and according to your original billing cycle method… You also agree that the amount billed for the renewal period may be different if your original subscription was purchased under a promotional offer, and you authorize us to charge your Payment Method for such varying amounts and any other charges that you may incur in connection with your subscription.” (Source: Scentbird Terms & Conditions, 2026)

That is the whole intro-offer mechanic in one sentence, and it is worth reading twice: the amount billed for the renewal period may be different if your original subscription was purchased under a promotional offer. The same document also notes that “Product prices may change in the course of your subscription” and that renewals land “on the 5th day of the month.”

We want to be careful here, because this is where competitor posts usually get sloppy and start implying something sinister. Introductory pricing is not deceptive by default, and it is not unique to Scentbird — we do it too. Base Note’s first month is $15 instead of $20. Our step-up is 33% rather than roughly 100%, which is a real difference in degree, but it is a difference in degree, not in kind. Any subscription that advertises a discounted first month is showing you a number you will only pay once. The honest test is not “is there an intro price” but “can you find the renewal price without hunting, and how big is the gap.”

For context on the legal baseline: the clearest US statute for online subscriptions is the Restore Online Shoppers’ Confidence Act, which the FTC summarises as prohibiting a post-transaction third-party seller from charging an account “unless it has disclosed clearly all material terms of the transaction and obtained the consumer’s express informed consent to the charge” (Source: Federal Trade Commission, 15 U.S.C. §§ 8401-8405). That specific provision targets third-party sellers rather than the merchant you signed up with, so it is background rather than an accusation. But “disclose all material terms clearly, get informed consent” is the standard the whole category gets measured against, and the renewal price is a material term.

Mechanism two: the surcharge you only meet after you are inside

This is the one that surprises people, because you generally do not encounter it until you are browsing the catalogue as a subscriber and fall for something expensive.

Scentbird runs two curated premium collections. On 18 August 2026, the Scentbird Select page showed “Now browsing 113 products,” and every single one of those 113 cards carried an added monthly charge printed under the “SCENTBIRD SELECT” label. We counted the whole page:

A second collection, Neiman’s Select, showed “Now browsing 51 products” on the same date, of which 48 displayed a surcharge label — 38 at +$10.00, 8 at +$15.00 and 2 at +$20.00 (Source: Neiman’s Select, 2026). The three remaining cards did not render a surcharge on the page we read, so we are not going to claim we know what they cost.

The houses behind those surcharges are the ones you would guess: Ex Nihilo, Vilhelm Parfumerie, Montale, Matiere Premiere, Fragrance Du Bois, Mancera. And here is the fair part — there is a real cost behind the surcharge. Niche houses sell at higher wholesale prices, produce in smaller runs, and often restrict who can distribute them. A subscription that charges one flat price across a 1,100-scent catalogue that spans both drugstore-adjacent designer and genuine niche has to solve that gap somehow: raise the base price for everybody, restrict what is available, or charge more for the expensive shelf. Scentbird chose the third. That is a legitimate answer to a real problem, not a trick. If you want the longer version of why niche costs more in the first place, we wrote designer vs niche on exactly that.

The problem is not the surcharge’s existence. It is that the surcharge is invisible at the moment you decide to subscribe, and it attaches to precisely the fragrances that made you want a fragrance subscription in the first place.

Five ways to avoid the jump

1. Write down the renewal price before you enter a card

Not the price on the button — the struck-through one next to it. If you cannot find a renewal price on a subscription checkout in under thirty seconds, that itself is information.

2. Check the surcharge label before you queue a scent, not after

On Scentbird the surcharge is printed on the product card, above the fragrance name. It is easy to miss because it sits where a category badge normally sits.

3. Know your billing date

Scentbird’s terms state that “Subscriptions renew at the end of your subscription term, on the 5th day of the month” (Source: Scentbird Terms & Conditions, 2026). Put it in your calendar with a two-day warning. That single reminder prevents most surprise charges in this category, ours included.

4. Do not assume referrals cut your own bill

This one catches people. Scentbird’s referral credits scale with tenure — “Customers in their first month receive one (1) free fragrance credit to share… Customers subscribed for six months or longer receive three (3)” — but the terms are explicit that they “are intended as a welcome gift for new customers and do not result in a credit being added to the referring subscriber’s own account” (Source: Scentbird Terms & Conditions, 2026). They are a gift you hand to a friend, not a discount you keep. Also worth noting: those credits “must be used before the end of that month” and do not roll over.

5. Understand the cancellation path before you need it

Per the same terms, add-on subscriptions such as Candle Club or Case Subscription can be cancelled yourself under Manage Subscription, but the fragrance subscription itself is different: “If you wish to terminate your automatic renewal for your fragrance subscription, you may do so by contacting our Customer Experience team through our Help Center.” Multi-month plans are stricter still — “for 3-, 6-, or 12-month subscription plans… cancellation will not take effect until the end of the applicable term. You will not receive a refund” (Source: Scentbird Terms & Conditions, 2026). If you are the kind of person who cancels things at 11pm without wanting to talk to anyone, that is a real consideration. Prepaying for a discount is also how you turn a $17.95 problem into a twelve-month one.
