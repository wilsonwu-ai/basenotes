What the 2026 price numbers mean for you

Retail Dive reported on August 24, 2026, citing the research firm Circana, that US prestige fragrance (the department-store and specialty-shop tier) sold about 6% more in dollars in the first half of 2026. Average prices rose about 5%, and unit sales were flat (read November 2026). In plain words, shoppers paid more per bottle and did not buy more bottles.

For you, that makes each purchase count for more. The same report says mass-market fragrance grew faster, up about 15% in dollars and 7% in units, so cheaper bottles are also selling. None of this says that the bottle you want went up by 5%. It describes the market as a whole.

Why buying unsmelled goes wrong

Interparfums CEO Jean Madar told Retail Brew on September 21, 2026 that people are now willing to buy without smelling, and that on TikTok Shop and Amazon you cannot smell. That was his statement about shoppers, and I am not adding to it. The usual causes are not mysterious.

  • Skin. Skin chemistry, meaning how your own skin and body warmth change a scent, differs from person to person. A scent that smells great on a reviewer can sit differently on you. Our post on why perfume smells different on you goes into it.
  • The opening versus the dry-down. The opening is what you smell in the first minutes. The dry-down is what is left after a few hours, and it is what you wear all day. A shop strip or a quick spray tells you about the first and almost nothing about the second. The words are explained in our guide to projection, sillage and longevity.
  • The setting. A scent smelled in a cool shop, on a sheet of paper, is not the same experience as a warm office or a crowded train.

We cover the general case for trying before buying in our earlier post on sampling. This post is about how to run the trial.

How big a sample you need

Two decant sellers' own buying guides, read in November 2026, say that samples of 0.5 to 2 ml run out before you understand the dry-down. They put about 5 ml as the size for a test over several weeks, and say a full bottle should wait for several good wears. That matches how I would do it. A 5 ml vial holds about 60 sprays, which is enough for the plan below with sprays to spare.

The two-week test plan

Use the same number of sprays in the same places each time, so the scent is the only thing that changes. I use 4 sprays as the standard and 3 on day 1, since you do not yet know how strong it is. If you want help picking your own number, see how many sprays of cologne to wear. Avoid other strong scents on test days: unscented soap and laundry help.

Day Setting Sprays Write down
1 At home, on skin, a quiet afternoon 3 What you smell at 15 minutes, 1 hour and 4 hours. Any note you did not expect.
2 Rest day, no scent from the vial 0 Whether you thought about it.
3 A work or busy day 4 Does it stay in the background? Do you notice it, or does it nag?
4 An evening out or a dinner 4 How it smells in a warm room, and whether it fits the occasion.
5 A warm day 4 Does heat make it sharp, sweet or heavy?
6 A cold day 4 Does it fade faster, or feel quieter, in the cold?
7 Rest day 0 Whether you miss it.
8 Repeat the setting you liked most 4 Is it as good the second time? Sometimes the day, not the scent, made the difference.
9 Ask one person you trust 4 Their reaction at arm's length and an hour later. Your nose tires, theirs has not.
10 Free day, no plan 4 or 0 Did you reach for it unprompted? Mark yes or no. Use the sprays only if yes.
11 Rest day 0 Same question as day 7.
12 Free day 4 or 0 Reached for it unprompted? Yes or no.
13 Free day 4 or 0 Reached for it unprompted? Yes or no.
14 Final check, on skin, a normal day 4 How it smells at hour 4. Is that the part you want to live with?

Total sprays: 3 on day 1, then 4 on each of days 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13 and 14. That is 3 + (10 x 4) = 43 sprays at most, which leaves about 17 in a 60-spray vial for a re-test. If the weather will not give you both a warm day and a cold day, swap them for whatever you get. The point is variety, not the exact temperature.

Keep notes short: one line each time, with the hour. "Still there at 4 hours, soft, a bit sweet" is enough. Notes made at the time are better than the memory you will have in two weeks.

The decision rule

Buy the bottle only if both of these are true:

  1. On days 10, 12 and 13, you reached for it unprompted on at least two of the three, and the rest days left you wanting it back.
  2. On day 1 and on day 14, you still liked how it smelled at hour four.

If either is false, keep the vial, finish it if you like, and move on to the next scent. A "no" after two weeks is a result. It cost you $20, not a bottle.

If you try several and keep liking more than one, that is a normal result. Building a small wardrobe may suit you better than hunting for one bottle.

When blind buying is reasonable

This is my opinion, not a rule. I think a blind buy is fine in two cases. One is when the bottle is cheap enough that being wrong is a shrug. The other is when it is a close relative of something you already own and love, such as a lighter or more intense version of a scent you know well. Even then I lean toward a vial first, because differences between versions are where surprises hide. A full-priced bottle in a style you have never worn is the case where I would not guess.
